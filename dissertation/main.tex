\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Financial sentiment systems commonly reduce a news item to a single label or score.
That compression is convenient, but it discards information about whether different models interpret the same text consistently.
This dissertation examines cross-model agreement as a separate, observable property of financial sentiment measurement.
It first tests whether agreement among a heterogeneous set of scorers varies with the graded human-annotation agreement recorded in Financial PhraseBank and whether lower agreement is associated with greater classification error.
It then applies the same fixed scorer roster to timestamped firm-level news from LSEG and asks whether agreement improves out-of-sample prediction of post-news abnormal returns beyond mean sentiment and the initial price reaction.
The market analysis uses a chronological development--evaluation split, separates the event-session reaction from subsequent cumulative abnormal returns, and compares nested predictive models on identical eligible events.
The design distinguishes benchmark competence, construct validity and external predictive validity; agreement is therefore not treated as a synonym for confidence, truth, reliability or investor crowding.

\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

Financial news is unstructured, abundant and difficult to compare across firms and dates.
Sentiment analysis addresses this problem by translating text into a small number of categories or a scalar score.
In a typical market application, article-level scores are aggregated and passed to a forecasting or trading model.
The resulting signal is easy to store and evaluate, but the compression conceals how the score was produced and whether alternative models read the same item in the same way.

This lost information matters because financial language is often context-dependent.
A sentence may describe an accounting gain that is economically weak, a loss that is smaller than expected, or guidance whose meaning depends on the named firm.
Models trained with different objectives or equipped with different domain knowledge need not resolve these cases identically.
A mean sentiment score can therefore be the same for an item on which all models agree and an item on which positive and negative judgements offset one another.
Treating those cases as equivalent assumes, rather than demonstrates, that disagreement has no practical value.

The distinction is relevant to both measurement and market analysis.
At the measurement level, agreement may identify items for which automated sentiment labels are more or less stable.
At the market level, agreement may add information about how a news event is subsequently resolved in prices.
These are separate propositions.
A relationship between model agreement and human annotation agreement would support convergent validity, but it would not establish that agreement predicts returns.
Likewise, predictive value in a particular news sample would not make agreement a causal measure of investor beliefs or positioning.

The dissertation therefore separates three questions that are easily conflated: whether the individual scorers are competent on a labelled benchmark; whether their agreement corresponds to human disagreement and error risk; and whether agreement contributes incremental out-of-sample information about post-news abnormal returns.
The separation is important because a system can classify benchmark sentences accurately without producing a useful market signal, and an ensemble can agree for reasons that have little to do with truth.

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}

The primary research question is:

\begin{quote}
Does cross-model agreement---assessed against graded human annotation agreement---add out-of-sample information about firm-level post-news abnormal returns beyond mean sentiment and the initial price reaction?
\end{quote}

Two supporting questions make the primary question testable:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{SQ\arabic*:}]
  \item Does agreement among the selected scorers increase with Financial PhraseBank annotator-agreement tiers and correspond to lower classification error?
  \item Does agreement improve held-out prediction of post-news abnormal returns relative to mean sentiment alone and to the strongest individual scorer?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

The intended contribution is a bounded empirical assessment of agreement rather than a broad claim that model consensus is inherently valuable.
The study makes three linked contributions.
First, it evaluates a deliberately heterogeneous roster of financial sentiment scorers on a provenance-clean benchmark.
Second, it tests item-level pairwise model agreement against the graded human-agreement structure of Financial PhraseBank rather than treating ensemble agreement as self-validating.
Third, it carries the same fixed construct into a timestamp-aware event study and measures its incremental predictive value on a chronological evaluation sample.

The design also contributes a reproducible evidence chain.
Dataset identities, prompt and model identifiers, content hashes, cohort exclusions, event-time rules and evaluation boundaries are recorded before the principal results are interpreted.
Null results remain informative: they can show that agreement describes measurement difficulty without improving market prediction, or that the selected agreement measure has limited validity in both settings.

\section{Scope}
\label{sec:scope}

The core study concerns three-class sentiment classification and daily firm-level abnormal returns.
It does not claim to identify investor beliefs, order flow, crowded positioning or causal price effects.
The market sample is restricted to the available LSEG US-company corpus and to securities with sufficient price history for the declared event-study model.
Daily prices cannot fully resolve intraday publication and reaction timing, so the event-session reaction is modelled separately and residual timing contamination is reported as a limitation.

Earlier work in this project explored threshold trading, index futures, reliability-weighted position sizing and writer--scorer effects.
Those extensions are not required to answer the present research question.
The valid exploratory threshold evidence is retained in \cref{app:exploratory}; the other designs are reserved for future work rather than presented as unfinished core chapters.

\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{ch:literature}

\section{Financial Text and Asset Prices}
\label{sec:lit-financial-text}

The use of text in finance begins from the observation that public information is not exhausted by structured variables.
News reports convey tone, uncertainty and context that can affect how market participants interpret an event.
\Textcite{tetlock2007} shows that pessimistic media content is associated with market activity and temporary price pressure, establishing both the relevance of textual measures and the possibility that an initial reaction can subsequently reverse.
\Textcite{tetlock2008words} extend the evidence beyond mood: the fraction of negative words in firm-specific news predicts earnings as well as returns, indicating that language can carry information about fundamentals rather than sentiment alone.
This literature motivates the use of post-event returns, but it does not imply that every automated sentiment score contains incremental predictive information.

Much empirical work converts text into a single polarity measure and relates that measure to contemporaneous or future returns.
This approach is attractive because the output is comparable across documents and can be inserted into conventional statistical models.
Its limitation is that it treats the scoring process as a black box.
The limitation is not only conceptual: \textcite{li2014news} find that polarity-only sentiment adds little to stock-return prediction in a five-year Hong Kong news sample, whereas richer sentiment representations built from domain dictionaries do.
If two models generate the same average score through different combinations of labels, the mean alone cannot reveal that distinction.
The present study asks whether the discarded cross-model variation is measurable and useful after the mean is already included.

\section{From Lexicons to Financial Language Models}
\label{sec:lit-sentiment-models}

Sentiment methods differ in the information they use.
Lexicon systems infer polarity from predefined word-level rules.
VADER, for example, combines a sentiment lexicon with rules for punctuation, emphasis, negation and degree modifiers \parencite{hutto2014vader}.
Such systems are transparent and inexpensive, but a general-purpose lexicon can misread financial expressions whose polarity depends on expectations or firm context.
\Textcite{loughran2011liability} document this problem directly: a large share of the words counted as negative by a widely used general dictionary, such as \emph{liability} or \emph{tax}, are not negative in a financial register, and finance-specific word lists materially change the resulting inference.
The general lesson is that the construct a sentiment system measures depends on the language its rules or parameters were formed on.

Domain-adapted language models address part of this problem by learning contextual representations from financial text.
FinBERT adapts a pretrained transformer to financial sentiment classification and is an important benchmark for testing whether general or generative models add value beyond a specialised classifier \parencite{araci2019finbert}.
Large language models offer greater flexibility and can follow target-specific prompts.
\Textcite{lopezlira2023chatgpt} report that ChatGPT-based news scores predicted next-day returns in their sample, particularly for smaller firms and negative news, and \textcite{kirtac2024llm} find that scoring with a large language model outperformed FinBERT and dictionary methods in a large-scale news-trading comparison.
Generative output nevertheless depends on training data, model version, prompt design and provider behaviour.
The comparison in this dissertation therefore uses fixed model identities and a fixed label mapping rather than treating all model outputs as interchangeable.

Backtests that score historical news with models trained on later data face an additional threat.
\Textcite{glasserman2023lookahead} use entity anonymisation to separate two in-sample effects in GPT sentiment backtests: look-ahead bias, in which the model may know how a story ended, and a distraction effect, in which background company knowledge pulls the model away from the text it is asked to score.
In their tests the distraction effect dominates, which warns against reading in-sample predictive fit as clean evidence in either direction.
\Textcite{sharma2026leakage} add counterevidence of a different kind: under a leakage-safe design, higher classification fidelity did not reliably translate into risk-adjusted trading value.
These results motivate the timestamp discipline, the chronological development--evaluation split and the staged separation of competence, validity and market value adopted here.

Model competence must be established before model agreement is interpreted.
High agreement among weak classifiers can simply reproduce a common error, while a heterogeneous group can disagree because its members respond to genuinely different aspects of the text.
Accuracy is also insufficient on its own when classes are imbalanced.
The benchmark analysis therefore reports macro-averaged F1 and class-level performance alongside accuracy, and it distinguishes parsing or request failures from valid predictions.

\section{Human Annotation and Measurement Difficulty}
\label{sec:lit-human-agreement}

Sentiment classification is usually evaluated against a single gold label, yet human annotators frequently disagree.
A growing body of natural-language-processing research argues that this label variation is signal rather than noise: it can encode ambiguity, legitimate differences in perspective or missing context \parencite{plank2022variation}.
\Textcite{pavlick2019inherent} show for textual inference that disagreement persists under careful data collection and is not an artefact removed by aggregation, and \textcite{uma2021disagreement} survey methods that learn from disagreement directly rather than discarding it.
An evaluation restricted to majority-label accuracy therefore misses part of the measurement problem.

Financial PhraseBank is particularly useful in this respect because its sentence labels are accompanied by subsets defined by annotator agreement \parencite{malo2014phrasebank}.
The 50, 66, 75 and 100 per cent tiers retain information that would be lost if only a single consensus label were used.
These tiers do not provide a perfect measure of ambiguity: disagreement can reflect annotator error, insufficient context or different reasonable readings.
Nevertheless, they offer an ordered external reference against which model agreement can be tested.

The central measurement expectation is modest.
If cross-model agreement is informative about item difficulty, it should tend to rise with human agreement and lower model agreement should tend to coincide with a greater risk of classification error.
Neither relationship is guaranteed.
Models may share training material or architectural biases, causing them to agree on mistakes, and the selected roster is a set of engineered systems rather than a random sample of independent raters.
For that reason, agreement is described as an observed property of the roster, not as reliability, confidence or truth.

Recent evidence extends this caution to large language models specifically.
\Textcite{ni2026reasoning} find across tasks and model configurations that the ability to represent annotator disagreement is an empirical property separate from classification accuracy, and one that reasoning interventions can improve or degrade.
Zero-shot models can likewise match dominant emotion labels while failing to reproduce the distribution of human uncertainty around them \parencite{inoshita2026emotion}.
These results support testing model agreement against the graded PhraseBank tiers explicitly rather than assuming that competent classifiers inherit the structure of human uncertainty.

Calibration research supplies a related warning.
Modern neural networks can be overconfident even when their class predictions are wrong \parencite{guo2017calibration}.
Confidence reported by one model and agreement observed across models are therefore distinct quantities.
The former concerns the model's own probability assignment; the latter concerns the similarity of outputs generated by the fixed roster.
This dissertation focuses on the latter and does not infer that agreement automatically produces calibrated probabilities.

\section{Disagreement and Financial Interpretation}
\label{sec:lit-disagreement}

Differences-of-opinion models show that heterogeneous interpretations can matter for trading and prices \parencite{harris1993differences}.
\Textcite{kandel1995differential} document that market participants appear to interpret the same public signal differently, which is the behavioural analogue of the quantity measured in this study.
\Textcite{hong2007disagreement} survey this literature and argue that disagreement models can jointly explain price and volume regularities that representative-agent models struggle to produce, with heterogeneous priors and short-sale constraints generating overpricing and changes in disagreement generating volume.
That theory helps explain why disagreement is worth measuring, but it does not license a direct mapping from model disagreement to investor disagreement.
The scorers do not observe positions, beliefs or order flow, and several may share training data or design conventions.

A second literature cuts the other way: it suggests that agreement among machine-learning systems may be systematically inflated.
\Textcite{kleinberg2021monoculture} show that many decision-makers adopting the same algorithm can be collectively costly even when that algorithm is individually superior, and \textcite{bommasani2022homogenization} document outcome homogenisation when different applications share a common foundation model.
For a sentiment roster, shared pretraining corpora, finance vocabulary and label conventions imply that high cross-model agreement can reflect a common error as easily as a clear text.
These results are conceptual analogies rather than market evidence, but they motivate two design choices in this study: the deliberately heterogeneous roster and the refusal to interpret agreement as truth.

Accordingly, the empirical market question is predictive rather than causal.
The analysis asks whether model agreement improves prediction of subsequent abnormal returns after mean sentiment and the initial reaction have been included.
A useful relationship may be consistent with several mechanisms, including textual clarity, event type, common model bias or market processing of the underlying news.
Distinguishing those mechanisms would require additional data and a different identification strategy.

\section{Machine Disagreement in Recent Financial Research}
\label{sec:lit-machine-disagreement}

The question studied here sits inside a rapidly forming literature that derives disagreement measures from panels of language models rather than from human forecasters or analysts.
\Textcite{liu2025disagreement} score earnings calls with six large language models and relate the resulting disagreement to established human and machine-learning disagreement measures, cross-sectional returns, trading volume and spreads.
\Textcite{bhagwat2026mirror} simulate a large panel of investor personas responding to several million firm-news headlines and find that the dispersion of simulated responses matches survey-based disagreement benchmarks and predicts abnormal trading volume.
On the classification side, agreement and divergence within a multi-model ensemble have been used as features that improve financial sentiment classification \parencite{zhang2025finsentllm}, and selective consensus among trading agents has been used as a decision filter intended to improve stability and risk-adjusted performance \parencite{li2026trusttrade}.

Two observations follow from this frontier.
First, cross-model disagreement in finance is no longer unclaimed territory, and this dissertation makes no first-use claim.
Second, the existing designs differ from the present one in instructive ways.
The earnings-call and persona studies validate their dispersion measures against other market or survey quantities rather than against graded human annotation of the scored text itself, and the ensemble studies treat agreement as a confidence or filtering device whose validity is assumed rather than tested.
None of the located studies combines construct validation against human-agreement tiers with a timestamp-aligned event study of post-initial-reaction abnormal returns.
Several of these papers are, at the time of writing, unreviewed working papers or preprints, so their findings are treated as indicative rather than settled.

\section{Event Studies and Predictive Evaluation}
\label{sec:lit-event-studies}

Event studies provide a standard framework for separating security returns associated with an event from broader market movements \parencite{mackinlay1997event}.
The key design decisions are the event timestamp, expected-return model, estimation window and event window.
In news research, publication timing is especially important because a calendar date does not necessarily identify the first tradable session after an article appeared.

Post-news horizons deserve particular care because both drift and reversal are documented.
\Textcite{chan2003news} finds drift after public news, especially bad news, but reversal after large price moves without identifiable news, and \textcite{savor2012information} reports the same information-conditional pattern for major price shocks.
\Textcite{tetlock2011stale} adds that investors react to stale, reprinted news, so the novelty of an item affects how its aftermath should be read.
These results imply that the meaning of a post-event window depends on whether an identifiable, novel news event anchors it.
That dependence motivates the timestamp-to-session rules and the earliest-revision canonicalisation applied to the LSEG corpus in \cref{ch:data}.

The present design separates the event-session abnormal return from the subsequent cumulative abnormal return.
This prevents the initial response from being silently included in the outcome that is meant to describe later resolution.
It also makes the predictive comparison explicit: mean sentiment and agreement are evaluated alongside the initial price reaction rather than being credited with information already visible in the event-session return.

Repeated model selection and multiple horizons can create apparently strong patterns by chance.
The broader asset-pricing literature emphasises this multiplicity problem \parencite{harvey2016multiple}.
Where families of inferential tests are reported, false-discovery control follows \textcite{benjamini1995fdr}; the primary predictive conclusion instead rests on a pre-specified held-out loss comparison with date-block bootstrap uncertainty.

\section{Research Gap}
\label{sec:lit-gap}

Existing research establishes that financial text carries return-relevant information, that human annotators disagree in structured and meaningful ways, and that disagreement among investors matters for prices and volume.
A recent frontier extends disagreement measurement to panels of language models and begins to relate it to markets.
Among the sources located in a targeted search conducted in July 2026, however, no study validates a cross-model agreement measure against graded human annotation agreement and then tests, on timestamp-aligned company news with a chronological evaluation split, whether the same fixed measure adds information about post-initial-reaction abnormal returns beyond mean sentiment and the initial reaction itself.
That positioning is search-bounded rather than a proof of absence, and it is deliberately narrower than a claim of first use.

The gap accordingly has two linked parts.
The measurement part asks whether agreement behaves as a valid indicator of item difficulty against the PhraseBank human-agreement tiers, including whether errors concentrate on low-agreement items and whether the pattern survives leave-one-model-out analysis.
The market part asks whether the identical fixed construct adds incremental out-of-sample information in an event study that separates the initial reaction from subsequent resolution.
Addressing only one part would be incomplete, and conflating them would repeat the pattern this review has criticised: treating agreement as confidence without validation, or as an investor quantity without evidence.
The methodology in \cref{ch:methodology} therefore uses the same fixed agreement construct in both settings and treats transfer between them as a hypothesis to test rather than an assumption.

\chapter{Data and Provenance}
\label{ch:data}

\section{Labelled Benchmark}
\label{sec:data-benchmark}

The default labelled dataset is \path{Data/derived/labeled/financial_sentiment_v2.csv}.
It is rebuilt by the repository's dataset-construction script from Financial PhraseBank and FiQA sources rather than edited manually.
The merged benchmark contains 5,947 observations.
The construct-validity analysis is restricted to the 4,836 Financial PhraseBank rows for which the original human-agreement tier can be recovered.
FiQA complements the classification benchmark but is not treated as if it supplied an equivalent annotation-agreement measure \parencite{maia2018fiqa}.

The repository also contains a legacy Kaggle-derived file, \texttt{Data/data.csv}.
That file is not used as the default benchmark because it contains documented merge corruption: 514 negative PhraseBank sentences were duplicated with an incorrect neutral label.
Those conflicts are data errors, not evidence of semantic ambiguity.
Excluding them prevents a provenance defect from being mistaken for the construct under study.

Every derived benchmark view records its source, cleaning and label mapping.
Stable row identity and content hashes are used when predictions from separate runs are joined.
The agreement analysis rejects content mismatches, duplicate identities and incomplete model coverage beyond the declared tolerance.

\section{LSEG News Corpus}
\label{sec:data-lseg}

The candidate market corpus comprises Reuters/LSEG news associated with 33 large US companies and covers approximately late December 2025 to June 2026.
The material is licensed local research data and is not reproduced in the dissertation.
Cleaning removes verified metadata and markup while preserving the original source files; derived corpora record the cleaner version and manifest identity.

The canonical item is the earliest eligible revision for each story-family--symbol pair.
This prevents repeated revisions of the same story from entering the scorer panel as apparently independent information.
After timestamp assignment, multiple eligible stories for the same symbol and event session are aggregated to one event observation before market inference.
All exclusions are recorded in an attrition table, including duplicate revisions, failed relevance checks, missing scores and unavailable return windows.

\section{Price Data}
\label{sec:data-prices}

The price panel contains each retained stock and a broad US market series.
It extends far enough before each event to estimate a market model over 120 sessions, followed by a 21-session gap, and far enough after the event to calculate the initial reaction and ten subsequent sessions.
Price and news identities are fixed before formal scoring of the evaluation cohort.

The use of daily data imposes a timing limitation.
Publication timestamps are converted from Coordinated Universal Time to New York time and mapped to a trading session under a declared close rule.
News published after that rule is assigned to the next session.
This procedure is more defensible than joining on calendar date alone, but it cannot identify the exact intraday sequence between publication and price adjustment.

\section{Scorer Roster}
\label{sec:data-roster}

The core roster combines four heterogeneous systems: ProsusAI/FinBERT, VADER, Cerebras \texttt{gemma-4-31b} and Cerebras \texttt{gpt-oss-120b}.
FinBERT supplies a domain-specific supervised model, VADER supplies a rule-based lexicon contrast, and the two hosted language models supply prompted generative classifiers.
The same four identities and label mapping are used for the PhraseBank and LSEG analyses.

LSEG items are scored with one target-company prompt, temperature zero and one sample.
The prompt hash, provider route, model identifier, content hash and runtime metadata are recorded for each formal run.
A failed request may be retried under the frozen retry policy; changing the prompt or model creates a new run identity rather than silently repairing an existing experiment.

\section{Development and Evaluation Samples}
\label{sec:data-split}

The LSEG event panel is divided chronologically.
Development data determine standardisation, the strongest individual scorer and all fitted coefficients.
The evaluation block is then used once for the nested predictive comparison described in \cref{sec:method-prediction}.
Identical eligible events are used for every compared model.
The period has informed earlier exploratory work, so it is described as a chronological evaluation block rather than as a pristine confirmatory holdout.

\chapter{Methodology}
\label{ch:methodology}

\section{Research Design}
\label{sec:method-design}

The empirical design has three stages.
First, each scorer is evaluated on the provenance-clean labelled benchmark to establish baseline competence.
Second, cross-model agreement is compared with Financial PhraseBank human-agreement tiers and classification error.
Third, the fixed agreement measure is transferred to the LSEG event panel and tested for incremental out-of-sample information about post-news abnormal returns.

This sequence prevents three different claims from being collapsed into one.
Benchmark performance concerns classification competence; association with human tiers concerns convergent construct validity; and held-out market performance concerns external predictive validity.
Success at an earlier stage does not guarantee success at a later one.

\section{Benchmark Evaluation}
\label{sec:method-benchmark}

Each valid prediction is mapped to the common negative, neutral or positive label set.
For scorer $j$, accuracy is the proportion of correct predictions and macro-F1 is the unweighted mean of the three class-specific F1 scores.
Balanced accuracy, Matthews correlation and class-level precision, recall and F1 are reported as supporting diagnostics.
Invalid outputs and request errors remain visible rather than being dropped from operational reporting.

The benchmark is used to establish competence and to select the strongest individual scorer using development evidence only.
It is not used to claim that a classifier predicts returns.
Where paired classifiers are compared on identical rows, the analysis reports their discordant outcomes and uses paired tests as supporting evidence.

\section{Cross-Model Agreement}
\label{sec:method-agreement}

For item $i$ and $m$ model labels, primary agreement is the fraction of model pairs that assign the same class:

\begin{equation}
  A_i =
  \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{j<k}\mathbb{1}(y_{ij}=y_{ik})}
       {\binom{m}{2}},
  \label{eq:agreement}
\end{equation}

where $y_{ij}$ is the label assigned to item $i$ by model $j$.
With four scorers, the denominator contains six pairs.
The continuous statistic is used in the principal analyses.
High, medium and low categories may be shown descriptively but do not replace the continuous measure or determine the primary result.

Agreement is kept separate from mean sentiment and score dispersion.
It is also separate from within-model sampling variability, which is unavailable in the one-sample core design.
Chance-corrected aggregate statistics such as Fleiss' $\kappa$ may describe the panel \parencite{fleiss1971kappa}, but item-level pairwise agreement is the pre-specified construct used in the validity and market tests.

\section{Construct-Validity Analysis}
\label{sec:method-validity}

The first supporting expectation is that model agreement increases with the ordered Financial PhraseBank human-agreement tier.
The primary analysis reports the Spearman rank association between $A_i$ and tier, together with bootstrap uncertainty and tier-specific means.
Sensitivity analyses adjust for gold class and text length because both can affect model performance and may be distributed unevenly across tiers.

Practical validity is examined by relating agreement to classification error.
The analysis reports consensus and individual-model error by tier and agreement level, as well as risk--coverage behaviour when low-agreement items are selectively withheld.
Leave-one-model-out calculations show whether the result depends on one scorer or persists across alternative three-model subsets.
These analyses test whether agreement is useful for this roster; they do not estimate a population parameter for all possible models.

\section{Event Definition and Timing}
\label{sec:method-events}

Each eligible LSEG item is converted from UTC to \texttt{America/New\_York} time and assigned to an event session under the frozen market-close rule.
The earliest eligible revision is retained for each story-family--symbol pair.
After scoring, items sharing a symbol and event session are aggregated so that the inferential unit is one symbol--session event rather than one article revision.

Let $R_{i,t}$ denote the return of security $i$ and $R_{m,t}$ the market return.
The expected-return model is estimated over 120 sessions ending 21 sessions before the event:

\begin{equation}
  R_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_i R_{m,t} + \varepsilon_{i,t}.
  \label{eq:market-model}
\end{equation}

Abnormal return is the observed return less the fitted market-model return:

\begin{equation}
  AR_{i,t} = R_{i,t} - \left(\widehat{\alpha}_i + \widehat{\beta}_i R_{m,t}\right).
  \label{eq:abnormal-return}
\end{equation}

The event-session abnormal return is stored as \texttt{initial\_reaction}.
The primary outcome is the cumulative abnormal return over the following five sessions:

\begin{equation}
  CAR_{i,+1:+5} = \sum_{h=1}^{5} AR_{i,t_i+h}.
  \label{eq:car}
\end{equation}

The event session is excluded from \cref{eq:car} by construction.
One- and ten-session post-event outcomes are pre-specified sensitivities.

\section{Held-Out Predictive Comparison}
\label{sec:method-prediction}

Three models are fitted on the development sample and evaluated on identical chronological evaluation events:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item the strongest individual sentiment scorer plus the initial reaction;
  \item ensemble mean sentiment plus the initial reaction;
  \item ensemble mean sentiment, continuous agreement, their interaction and the initial reaction.
\end{enumerate}

In notation, the agreement-augmented model is

\begin{equation}
  CAR_{i,+1:+5} =
  \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 \overline{S}_i + \gamma_2 A_i
  + \gamma_3\left(\overline{S}_i A_i\right)
  + \gamma_4 IR_i + u_i,
  \label{eq:predictive-model}
\end{equation}

where $\overline{S}_i$ is ensemble mean sentiment and $IR_i$ is the initial abnormal reaction.
Standardisation and fitted parameters use development data only.
The predictive specification does not use date fixed-effect categories that would be unavailable for new evaluation dates.

The primary estimand is the weighted evaluation loss difference

\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{\mathrm{MSE}} =
  \operatorname{MSE}(\text{mean + agreement})
  - \operatorname{MSE}(\text{mean only}).
  \label{eq:mse-difference}
\end{equation}

A negative value favours the agreement-augmented model.
Mean absolute error and out-of-sample $R^2$ are supporting metrics.
A date-block bootstrap supplies uncertainty for the loss difference while preserving dependence among firms exposed to the same market date.
Coefficient estimates are secondary explanatory evidence and are not substituted for held-out predictive performance.

\section{Feasibility, Robustness and Decision Rules}
\label{sec:method-robustness}

The market route proceeds only if at least 80 per cent of evaluation events align to valid return windows, at least 25 symbols survive, audited relevance precision is at least 0.85, and the sample spans enough independent dates for the declared block inference.
The end-to-end dry run must also demonstrate that the initial reaction is excluded from the post-event outcome.
If these conditions fail, the primary dissertation route becomes measurement validity and the LSEG analysis is reported as exploratory.

Robustness analyses include the one- and ten-session outcomes, alternative agreement descriptions, leave-one-model-out agreement and a full attrition audit.
Choices are not changed after evaluation outcomes are inspected.
Any departure from the frozen protocol is dated and reported with its reason.

\chapter{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{ch:discussion}

\section{Measurement Value and Market Value}
\label{sec:discussion-measurement-market}

The central interpretive distinction is between measurement value and market value.
If agreement tracks PhraseBank tiers or error risk, it can help identify sentences on which automated classification is more fragile.
That use may be practically important even if agreement does not improve return prediction.
Conversely, a market association without construct validity would require caution because the agreement statistic could be proxying for class balance, event type or a shared model bias rather than interpretable textual difficulty.

\section{Interpretation of Agreement}
\label{sec:discussion-interpretation}

Agreement is conditional on the chosen roster.
The four systems are heterogeneous in architecture and training objective, but they are not independent draws from a well-defined model population.
Shared public training corpora, common label conventions and similar language-model components can create correlated errors.
High agreement can therefore indicate a clear item, a dominant class cue or a common mistake.

The market analysis is associational.
It does not observe investor beliefs, holdings or trades and cannot establish that model agreement represents market consensus or crowded positioning.
The interaction between mean sentiment and agreement describes how the predictive relationship changes within this sample; it does not identify the behavioural mechanism producing that change.

\section{Data and Timing Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion-data}

Financial PhraseBank consists of short financial sentences and predates the hosted models used in the study.
Its human-agreement tiers provide a valuable external reference, but validity established there may not transfer fully to longer Reuters/LSEG stories, target-company prompts or recent market events.
Benchmark contamination is also possible because model training data are not completely observable.
These limitations make the LSEG test necessary while preventing it from being described as a clean causal validation.

The LSEG sample covers a limited period and company universe.
News events cluster by firm and calendar date, and market-wide shocks can affect many observations at once.
The date-block bootstrap addresses part of this dependence, but it does not create information absent from the available dates.
Daily prices leave residual intraday contamination even after timestamp conversion and session assignment.

\section{Modelling and Evaluation Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion-models}

The strongest-scorer choice, standardisation and coefficients are estimated on the development sample, yet the chronological evaluation period has been touched by earlier exploratory work.
Calling it a pristine holdout would overstate the design.
The dissertation instead reports the prior exploration and treats the frozen evaluation as a disciplined out-of-sample comparison within the available project history.

Predictive loss is sensitive to rare large returns, weighting and the finite number of independent dates.
For that reason, MSE is accompanied by MAE, out-of-sample $R^2$, a block-bootstrap interval and pre-specified horizon sensitivities.
Robustness results are supporting evidence; they are not used to replace an unfavourable primary result.

\section{From Event Study to Deployment}
\label{sec:discussion-deployment}

Incremental prediction in an event study is not equivalent to deployable alpha.
A trading strategy would additionally require a tradable publication-time rule, realistic execution prices, transaction costs, position sizing, capacity controls and evidence across regimes.
It would also need protection against model-version drift and changes in the news distribution.
The core dissertation deliberately stops at the predictive question unless the pre-specified extension gate permits one fixed economic comparison.

\section{Contribution and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion-future}

The study's contribution is to test agreement as a separate model-output property rather than assuming that an ensemble mean contains all relevant information.
Its staged design clarifies whether agreement is useful for benchmark error detection, market prediction, both or neither.

Natural extensions include intraday event timing, broader markets and periods, richer model rosters, prompt and sampling variation, and direct comparison with human uncertainty on contemporary company news.

Any economic extension should begin from a frozen predictive result and add realistic execution and cost assumptions.

\chapter{Exploratory Threshold-Strategy Evidence}
\label{app:exploratory}

This appendix preserves the valid evidence from the earlier Layer-1 trading study while separating it from the refocused agreement research question.
The exercise evaluated a threshold rule on an eight-company exploratory panel and helped establish leakage controls, chronological tuning and multiplicity-aware reporting.
It is hypothesis-generating and is not part of the held-out LSEG comparison in \cref{ch:results}.

\section{Trading Rule}

For company $i$ on news date $t$, the exploratory signal $S_{i,t}$ was the arithmetic mean of per-article labels in $\{-1,0,+1\}$.
Given no-trade band $L\geq0$, the position was

\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{position}_{i,t}=
  \begin{cases}
    +1, & S_{i,t}\geq L \text{ and } S_{i,t}>0,\\
    -1, & S_{i,t}\leq-L \text{ and } S_{i,t}<0,\\
    0,  & \text{otherwise.}
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}

Entry used the adjusted open of the first observed session after the news date, never the same session, and exit used the adjusted close one to seven sessions later.
Events were summarised as equal-weight event returns rather than as a funded portfolio because company-day windows overlapped.

The band, horizon and minimum-article gate were swept on a chronological training split only.
Across the 28 equal-weight parameter cells in the six-date backfill panel, no cell achieved a positive training-split Sharpe ratio.
The selected least-negative training cell had $L=0$ and a two-session horizon; its held-out Sharpe ratio of 0.66 was based on only eleven test events and was not treated as reliable evidence.
A conviction-weighted variant performed no better on held-out data.
