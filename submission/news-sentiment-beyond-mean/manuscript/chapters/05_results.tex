Results follow the six methodology steps. Threshold and story-count checks come
after the temporal test because they affect both periods.

\section{Steps 1 and 2: aggregation comparison and controlled estimate}

Only negative-story share remains statistically significant after correcting
the nine training-period comparisons, and the relationship is small and
negative. It is the only rule in
\cref{tab:aggregation-family,fig:aggregation-ic} to survive the adjustment for
nine comparisons: its mean daily rank correlation is $-0.00543$, with HAC
$t=-3.12$ and $p=0.0018$. The hard-label and continuous-score means are near
0.003, both with $p>0.09$. Median sentiment and decayed state have only
uncorrected $p$-values below 0.05. Non-mean summaries do not outperform as a
group. The comparison also cannot explain why negative-story share alone
survived correction.

The relationship remains negative after accounting for average sentiment and
story count. Across 512,149 firm-days and 2,264 sessions, the controlled
negative-share coefficient is $-0.00831$ (SE $0.00295$, 95\% CI
$[-0.01410,-0.00252]$, $p=0.0049$). It clears the simple nine-test Bonferroni
threshold of 0.00556.

The same training data helped select negative share before the model was
estimated. This interval therefore understates selection uncertainty, and the
estimate may be inflated.

The coefficient describes an ordering, not a basis-point return. Within a
session, higher negative-share rank is associated with lower assigned-window
return rank after controlling for mean sentiment and $\log(1+n)$. Adding
one- and five-session return ranks plus 20-session volatility gives $-0.00914$
(SE $0.00282$, 95\% CI $[-0.01466,-0.00362]$, $p=0.00118$) on price-complete
rows. \Cref{tab:price-controls} shows the nested models.

In the full model, the one-session return and volatility controls are both
negative, while the mean-sentiment coefficient is close to zero
(\cref{tab:price-controls}). Negative share remains negative after these controls
are added. The beta-adjusted coefficient is $-0.01054$, or $-0.00950$ after
controlling for beta rank; both survive correction.

Ties restrict the observed negative-share-rank spread to 0.56056 between the
pooled 90th and 10th percentiles. The full-control translation is therefore
$0.56056\times-0.00914=-0.00512$, or $-0.512$ return-rank percentile points.
Using the mean within-session spread gives $-0.482$ points. Both are small.

\section{Step 3: where the association appears in the return window}
\label{sec:outcome-leg-decomposition}

The window split in \cref{tab:outcome-leg-decomposition} changes the timing
interpretation. The assigned-open-to-close coefficient is $-0.01029$ (95\% CI
$[-0.01596,-0.00462]$, BH $q=0.0011$), while close-to-next-open is $+0.00155$
($[-0.00416,+0.00725]$, $q=0.595$). The training association is intraday, not in
the later overnight leg that more plausibly follows the day's news.

Pre-open returns do not resolve the problem. Previous-close-to-assigned-open is
$-0.00638$ (unadjusted $p=0.0457$, BH $q=0.0686$). The separate
previous-open-to-assigned-open probe is $-0.01926$ (95\% CI
$[-0.02580,-0.01272]$, $p=7.9\times10^{-9}$). Stock and SPY return identities
reconcile within $1.8\times10^{-15}$, and the reconstructed abnormal return
matches the baseline panel exactly.

\input{artifacts/tab_outcome_leg_decomposition}

The FNSPID data cannot place each story before or after the assigned open, so
they do not support a claim that the effect is specific to next-open
returns. The intraday coefficient
may be contemporaneous. Assignment lag or earlier information may explain the
previous-open result. Aggregate data cannot separate these explanations or
quantify their role in the story-count and period patterns.

The post-result measurement checks in
\cref{tab:outcome-label-sensitivities} carry no timing claim. The hard-share
coefficient is $+0.00027$ (BH $q=0.929$) on close-to-close returns and $+0.00182$
($q=0.815$) when the outcome is the trailing FF3 residual of those returns. Mean
FinBERT $p(\mathrm{negative})$ is $-0.00378$ on the primary outcome ($q=0.337$).
None survives correction.

\section{Step 4: the fixed 2020--2023 test}
\label{sec:temporal-replication}

The fixed test does not repeat the training-period relationship: the unchanged
coefficient changes sign. Across 203,393 testing firm-days and 998 sessions, the
all-firm-day coefficient is $+0.00583$ (95\% CI
$[-0.00426,+0.01593]$, $p=0.257$, BH $q=0.515$). The pre-specified multi-story
estimate is $+0.00295$ ($[-0.00990,+0.01581]$, $q=0.653$). None of the nine
information coefficients survives correction, so H1b is not supported. Under
the pre-specified decision rule, no alternative aggregator is selected and the
signal is not reversed.

The pre-specified testing-minus-training contrast is $+0.01414$ (SE $0.00594$,
95\% CI $[+0.00250,+0.02579]$, $p=0.0173$). It remains statistically significant
with HAC lags from 0 to 42. The daily coefficient series shows almost no serial
dependence, and Ljung--Box tests through lag 21 find no evidence of serial
correlation. The contrast is therefore unlikely to be caused by the chosen lag
length.

Changing the market benchmark does not remove the contrast. It is $+0.01884$
after trailing-beta adjustment and $+0.01628$ with beta rank controlled; both
survive correction. Unit market beta does not explain the period difference.

The window split does not locate the change. Testing-period intraday and
post-close coefficients are small and positive, while the pre-open overnight
coefficient is negative and survives correction. None of the corresponding
between-period contrasts survives correction. These additional tests do not
identify one return leg as the source of the instability.

\Cref{fig:coefficient-drift} places both periods on one axis. Annual means are
negative from 2011 to 2018 and non-negative from 2019 onward. Because 2019 is
the final training year, the sign change does not align with the fixed boundary
and starts before COVID-19. Every annual interval crosses zero, so the figure
identifies neither a break date nor its cause.

The figure is descriptive: annual estimates are uncertain and show neither a break date nor a cause. Source: aggregate Notebook 75 daily coefficient series.}

The test had limited ability to detect a small negative relationship. Pre-test
power was 35.4\%, with an 80\%-power MDE of 0.01372 at the corrected two-test
threshold. The observed HAC error was 15.8\% larger, implying 26.5\% power and an
MDE of 0.01588. The failed test therefore cannot establish a zero effect. Winner
selection and sampling variation may contribute to the discrepancy, but the data
do not identify the cause. The positive sign also cautions against transferring
\citet{garcia2013}'s aggregate recession result directly to this firm-level
design.

\section{Step 5: transaction costs}

Before costs, the negative-share portfolio earns $0.890$ basis points per
session on one-way turnover of 0.712 (\cref{fig:break-even}). A 10-basis-point
per-side charge costs 14.24 basis points and leaves $-13.35$ basis points net.
The descriptive break-even point is $0.625$ basis points per side. Across nine
rules it ranges from $0.116$ to $0.625$, making the assumed charge 16.0 times
the best point estimate.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{fig_break_even_cost_gap}
  \caption[Break-even costs against the assumed charge]{Per-side break-even transaction-cost point estimates for nine FNSPID rules on a logarithmic scale. The shaded band is the gap between the best estimate and the 10-basis-point charge. Source: aggregate Notebook 03 outputs.}
  \label{fig:break-even}
\end{figure}

Learned no-trade filters do not solve the cost problem. Testing AUCs are
0.5005--0.5038, and net performance is 7.72--16.89 basis points below the fixed
band. Gradient-boosted and logistic losses exceed their nominal MDEs. The MLP's
7.72-basis-point loss remains imprecise against a 10.04-basis-point MDE. The
shuffled-label control's simulated wealth falls below zero, so later performance
measures are undefined. H3 fails for every implementation.

\section{Step 6: same-session risk tests}

The news modifier does not show a clear overall advantage over the volatility
benchmark once their different levels of market exposure are considered.

In the 2020--2023 same-session comparison, the negative-pressure state raises
HAR cumulative net return from 29.85\% to 35.20\% and lowers mean exposure from
0.619 to 0.569. The 0.366-basis-point average return advantage is imprecise, while
squared downside falls ($p=0.0148$). Maximum drawdown worsens from $-15.73\%$ to
$-16.36\%$, despite improving in 2020 from $-12.95\%$ to $-7.39\%$.

An after-the-fact constant HAR multiplier matches the overlay's realised mean
exposure. The overlay gains 0.592 basis points per session against this
descriptive comparator (95\% block-bootstrap CI $[-0.635,+1.962]$, $p=0.3712$).
The interval crosses zero, and the comparator was not selected in training.

Almost the entire advantage in \cref{fig:har-equity} arises during the first ten
weeks of 2020. The overlay cuts exposure during the COVID-19 crash and avoids
part of the HAR portfolio's fall to a net-equity index of 0.90. The paths then
run almost parallel, and the overlay gives back part of that lead in 2022.

The news rule ends at 1.352 against the base at 1.298. It uses news assigned to the current session and is not a one-session-lagged trading signal. Source: Notebook 24.}

\Cref{fig:downside-placebo,fig:risk-regime} first test whether the risk-off
schedule aligns unusually with downside days. They do not show whether the news
was known before returns began. About 1\% of non-zero circular shifts match the
observed FNSPID 2020--2023 squared-downside reduction. Across the three tested
periods (FNSPID 2013--2019, FNSPID 2020--2023 and LSEG/Gemma 2025--2026), only
FNSPID 2020--2023 remains unusual after correction (BH $q=0.0301$).

A separate concentration check omits each of the 44 FNSPID 2020--2023 risk-off
episodes in turn. Every resulting confidence interval for the squared-downside
reduction remains above zero. The interval also remains above zero after
excluding February--April 2020 ($p=0.0034$). Although the two largest episodes
contribute 30.1\% and 14.3\% of the total reduction, neither one episode nor the
first COVID-19 crash explains the whole result.

a same-session relationship because current-session news sets exposure under the

rule never activates in the later LSEG period. It has no demonstrated timing or
economic advantage based on information known in advance.

\section{The LSEG analysis}

Both primary LSEG coefficients are negative, but they use a different data
source and return definition from FNSPID. The earlier-period estimate is $-0.02861$
(95\% CI
$[-0.07283,+0.01560]$, BH $q=0.409$). The later-period estimate is $-0.01573$
($[-0.09453,+0.06308]$, $q=0.696$). Their 80\%-power MDEs are 7.6 and 13.6 times
the FNSPID baseline. The samples cannot show whether the relationship repeats
in another source
(\cref{fig:cross-source-power}).

An earlier 44-firm LSEG comparison also produces no BH survivor in either of its
two families: the 18-test aggregator/scorer family and the eight-test
scorer/filter family. It is reported separately because it uses different firms.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{fig_cross_source_coefficient_power}
  \caption[Cross-period and cross-source coefficients]{Negative-share estimates after the main controls, with 95\% HAC intervals. The fixed FNSPID test uses 2020--2023. LSEG uses raw returns rather than FNSPID market-adjusted returns, so the data sources are never combined. LSEG labels show its 80\%-power MDE as a multiple of the FNSPID coefficient. Sources: aggregate Notebooks 71, 75, 77 and 79.}
  \label{fig:cross-source-power}
\end{figure}

The FNSPID risk rule never fires in the later period: its maximum $z$-score is
1.349 against an entry threshold of 1.5. It fires on 53 of 456 sessions in the
earlier period. Downside and shift placement survive correction, but the return
difference is 0.144 basis points (95\% CI $[-1.295,+1.688]$), and two of eighteen
episodes provide 65.6\% of the reduction. This does not show that the result
repeats in another source.

The aggregate search compared 6,672 combinations of news-pressure measure,
historical normalisation, lookback window, risk-off mapping, threshold and
exposure multiplier. All comparisons used three chronological 152-session
training folds. One aggregate rule was selected from those folds and evaluated
once, unchanged, in the later 167-session testing period. Its testing difference
is $-0.049$ basis points (95\% CI $[-0.656,+0.633]$).

A separate firm-level search compared 4,608 company-level rules using the 445
complete training sessions described in \cref{ch:data}. The selected rule was
then evaluated in the same 167-session testing period. It reduces losses against
full investment but loses to training-selected constant company weights, with
0.63 percentage points more drawdown, 0.27 percentage points more downside
deviation and 25.9 times the turnover (\cref{fig:lseg-matched-value}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{fig_lseg_matched_control_value}
  \caption[LSEG matched-control economic value]{Firm-level LSEG loss control over the 167-session testing period. The news rule reduces losses compared with full investment, but a constant rule with the same average exposure performs better. Per US\$1 million, the rule avoids US\$24,738 of losses but forgoes US\$41,126
of gains. It also incurs US\$12,715 of costs. A US\$1,767 compounding residual
reconciles the US\$30,870 ending-wealth gap. Every pre-specified economic
criterion fails, so H4 fails.

Taken together, H1a is consistent with the training estimate, subject to
selection and timing. H1b is not supported in testing, H2 has the declared sign
but is imprecise, and H3--H4 miss their economic criteria. The evidence
establishes neither causality, pre-return availability nor cost coverage.
