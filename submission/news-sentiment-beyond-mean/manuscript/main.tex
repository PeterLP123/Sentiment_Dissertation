\section*{Abstract}
\phantomsection
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Financial-news models often average all stories about a company on a given day,
discarding their distribution. This dissertation tests whether negative-story
share, the fraction classified as negative, adds information beyond that mean.

I use FinBERT, a language model for financial sentiment, to score news for more
than 700,000 US firm-days. A firm-day is one company on one trading day. I
compare nine daily summaries during the 2011--2019 training period, then test the
selected measure after controlling for mean sentiment, news volume, recent
returns and market exposure. The 2020--2023 testing period asks whether the
relationship persists. Separate Reuters/LSEG data test whether it appears in
another source, while trading costs and risk checks assess practical value.

Negative-story share alone survives correction across the nine training
comparisons. A higher share is associated with lower returns after the main
controls, but the effect is small and was selected in the same data. It appears
during the assigned session and in an earlier window, but not after the close.
Without exact story arrival times, the analysis cannot tell whether the news
predicted returns, arrived during the return window or was assigned to the wrong
session.

Because 48.3\% of training firm-days contain one story and estimates on
multi-story days are inconclusive, the result does not establish that a
mix of stories within a day drives the association.

In the fixed 2020--2023 test, the estimate changes sign. The daily rule cannot
cover plausible costs, and the risk rule does not reduce maximum drawdown, the
largest fall from a previous peak. The Reuters/LSEG estimates are too uncertain
to show that the result appears in another source. The evidence therefore shows
a small training-period association with uncertain timing that does not persist
or support a practical trading rule.
